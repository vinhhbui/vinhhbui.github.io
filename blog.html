Designing a rip current detection system for real rescue workflows

Field note7 min readDrone systems

Rip currents are dangerous because they can be hard to see from shore. The technical goal is not only to draw a box on video, but to create an early-warning system that helps lifeguards make decisions quickly.

My approach starts with the environment. Water reflections, moving waves, weather changes, and camera motion all create patterns that can confuse a model. That makes data quality and testing conditions just as important as architecture choice.

What I optimize for

I care about speed, stability, and clear output. A drone system has limited compute, so the model has to be lightweight enough for edge inference while still being reliable under messy coastal conditions.

The useful system is the one that works when the scene is imperfect, not only when the demo video is clean.

The next step is connecting model output to a human workflow: alert timing, location clarity, and confidence signals that are understandable during rescue operations.

How I approach YOLO experiments before training gets expensive

5 min readModel workflowDataset quality

Before I train a YOLO model, I try to remove avoidable uncertainty. A few hours spent inspecting labels can save many failed training runs later.

My checklist

I check class balance, annotation consistency, image resolution, lighting variation, and whether the train/validation split represents the real deployment scene. If the validation set is too easy, the metrics become comforting but not useful.

I also prefer small baseline runs before large experiments. They reveal broken labels, over-aggressive augmentations, and bad assumptions about the data pipeline while the cost is still low.

Good experiments are not just faster. They make wrong assumptions visible earlier.

Only after that do I compare model sizes, augmentation strategies, and inference settings for the deployment target.

What full-stack projects taught me about AI product interfaces

4 min readWeb developmentProduct thinking

Building hotel management dashboards, startup platforms, and FaceFeel taught me that AI projects still depend on ordinary product details: loading states, permissions, error messages, and database design.

An accurate model can feel weak if the interface does not show what is happening. A simpler model can feel strong when the workflow is clear and users know what to do next.

What changed in my thinking

I now design AI features around the decision they support. For a recommender, that means feedback and context. For detection systems, that means timing, confidence, and visual clarity. For dashboards, that means the right people seeing the right data.

The interface is where machine learning becomes usable.

That is why I keep practicing both sides: model development and full-stack implementation.

How I keep computer vision learning notes useful

6 min readStudy workflowComputer Vision

Learning notes only help when they survive beyond the day they were written. For computer vision, I try to keep notes close to experiments, code snippets, failure cases, and visual examples.

My preferred format is simple: the idea, the setup, the result, what confused me, and what I would test next. That structure works for papers, model training logs, AR demos, and small prototypes.

What makes a note reusable

A reusable note has enough context that future me can restart the experiment without guessing. It includes dataset assumptions, library versions when they matter, screenshots or outputs, and one clear takeaway.

A good learning note is not a diary entry. It is a small bridge back into the problem.

This keeps the blog useful too: each post should capture a lesson that can guide the next build.
